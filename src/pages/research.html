How rocks record a changing Earth.

Our long-term aim is to understand how sediments preserve and distort environmental change, so that we can reconstruct Earth’s history more reliably.

We work across field geology, carbonate geochemistry, and quantitative modeling. A central challenge connects our projects: different processes can leave similar geological records. We test which explanations fit the evidence, and what additional observations would distinguish them.

What do ancient coastlines tell us about sea level and ice sheets?

Fossil reefs and former shorelines preserve evidence of times when sea level differed from today. Their interpretation depends on when they formed, where they sat relative to the water, and how the land subsequently moved.

A shoreline can also advance as reefs grow and sediment fills shallow water. We connect rock layers and landforms across a landscape to test which patterns require changing sea level and which can arise from sediment redistribution.

Our developing research in Turks and Caicos combines mapping and 3D surveys with models of reef growth, waves, sediment transport, and landscape evolution. Work across the Bahamas and Pacific connects local records to broader histories of ice-sheet change.

When did sediments form, and how did they become rock?

A grain may form long before it is deposited. Minerals that cement grains together may grow later. Subsequent chemical exchange can change the isotope record again.

We are developing research on how uranium and thorium isotopes evolve through these processes. The aim is to establish when grains and cements in beach, dune, and seafloor deposits provide useful ages or limits on deposition.

Field relationships, microscopy, isotope measurements, and statistical models will test those histories together. Even where reliable ages cannot be recovered, the chemistry can help explain how sediments hardened and altered.

How faithfully do rocks record Earth’s carbon cycle?

Carbonate chemistry provides evidence about ancient oceans and environments. But a chemical change through a rock sequence can also reflect local sediment sources, transport, or reactions after deposition.

Our work combines stratigraphic context with models of physical and chemical processes to distinguish these possibilities. Research on Neoproterozoic and Paleozoic records asks how local effects influence signals often interpreted as global carbon-cycle change.

Reconstructing a shared signal also requires a timeline. With StratMC, we jointly consider age uncertainty, incomplete sections, and differences among records. Stacey Edmonsond’s work applies this approach to the Lomagundi–Jatuli carbon-isotope excursion and its relationship to early Earth’s oxygenation.

Our connection to HiSEAS.

HiSEAS investigates when, how much, and why ice sheets melted during the Last Interglacial. The collaboration connects sea-level observations, climate archives, and models of ice sheets and the solid Earth.

Blake is a collaborator contributing carbonate field geology, sedimentology, and statistical expertise. The 2026 Cook Islands campaign connects this field evidence to the broader project.

HiSEAS is supported by U.S. National Science Foundation award EAR 24-20482 (2024–2029).
